Overall Message
A good experiment section does not need to be complicated.
It needs to be explicit.
• What changes?
• What stays the same?
• What is measured?
• When is it measured?
Many imperfect answers contain 
strong research intuition.

Q4 Control Group
Example

Q5 Outcomes
Example
The primary outcome is the percentage of the $10,000 endowment allocated to the risky 
asset (0–100%).
Why this works
• Concrete and measurable
• Directly records portfolio choice
• Measured before market luck appears
Next improvement
• Lower risk is not always better
• Match choice to risk tolerance
• Add comprehension as secondary 
outcome
Q5 Outcomes
Example
在经过两周后，测量比较两组参与者的投资回报率，观察投资回报率是否在风险信息可视
化后得到提高。
Useful idea: students want real investment consequences.
Revision point: future realized return may reflect market luck, not 
better risk understanding.
Q5 Better Outcomes
➢Risk exposure: % allocated to risky assets
➢Risk-tolerance alignment: chosen risk vs pre-measured tolerance
➢Diversification: number of assets or HHI
➢Ex ante Sharpe ratio: based on information available at choice
➢Risk comprehension: quiz or estimated probability of loss
The primary outcome is the match between chosen portfolio risk and 
pre-measured risk tolerance.

Beyond the Six Questions
•External validity: would the result matter outside this experiment?
•Baseline information: what should be measured before treatment?
•Blinding and demand effects: can participants guess the hypothesis?
Do not add advanced terms unless they help the reader trust the 
design.
External Validity
• Sample: finance students or ordinary young investors?
• Task: hypothetical allocation or realistic platform behavior?
• Setting: online survey, simulation, or brokerage app?
• Time: immediate response or persistent behavior?
The more realistic the setting is, the harder it is to control.
Baseline Information
• Demographics: describe sample and check balance
• Investment experience: screen participants
• Financial literacy: balance and mechanism
• Risk preference: stratification and outcome construction
• Visual/numerical ability: who benefits from charts?
• Attention checks: exclude invalid responses
Baseline information is not decoration.
Blinding and Demand Effects
• Participants can usually tell whether they saw a chart or text.
• The bigger concern: can they guess the hypothesis?
• Use a neutral title: “A study on financial decision-making.”
• Automate assignment and material presentation.
• Anonymize group labels during analysis when possible.
Do not casually write “double-blind” unless you can explain what is blinded.
Model Experiment Section
• Participants: young investors aged 18–30 with limited experience.
• Random assignment: assigned at the individual level.
• Treatment: changes only the way risk information is presented.
• Control: same underlying risk information in text.
• Outcome: match between chosen risk and risk tolerance.
• Timing: immediately after portfolio submission.
Borrow the sentence structure, not necessarily the exact 
design.
Revision Exercise
• Choose one useful idea from your own answer.
• Write the clean comparison in one sentence.
• Name one primary outcome.
• Say when the outcome is measured.
• Add one sentence about external validity or demand effects.
Revision is not rejection. It is how a good intuition 
becomes a credible design.
A credible experiment requires
more than random assignment.
We also need to justify the participants, the task, the 
outcome, the timing, and whether the result can generalize.
Clear design first. Sophistication second.
