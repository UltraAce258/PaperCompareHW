The Research Question
Observation: The Anomalies did not fit CAMP
The Puzzle: CAPM says only beta should matter. But these variables all seem 
to matter. Why?
Question:
"Since E/P, ME, leverage, and BE/ME are all scaled versions of price, it is 
reasonable to expect that some of them are redundant for describing 
average returns. Our goal is to evaluate the joint roles of market β, size, E/P, 
leverage, and book-to-market equity in the cross-section of average 
returns." — Fama & French (1992), p. 428
Their Core Question:
Which of these variables truly matter, and which are just proxies for others?
Where Did Fama-French Get Their Idea?
They built on prior anomalies literature, but their innovation was:
Synthesis: Previous papers each found one anomaly. Fama-French asked: 
"Which ones are real when we test them together?”
Methodological insight: Previous tests used portfolios sorted on one variable. 
Fama-French used double sorting to separate correlated effects.
Parsimony: They sought the simplest model that captures everything.
The “Ah! So that explains it! Moment
"The greatest compliment is 'Ah! So that explains it!'" — Varian
Before Fama-French:
Finance profession believed: Beta explains everything
But: Many anomalies kept appearing
Problem: No one knew which anomalies were real vs. redundant
The “Ah! So that explains it! Moment
After Fama-French:
"Ah! So size and value capture all those anomalies!"
"Ah! That's why beta seems to work sometimes—it's correlated with size!" 
“Our main result is that for the 1963-1990 period, size and book-to-market equity 
capture the cross-sectional variation in average stock returns associated with size, E/P, 
book-to-market equity, and leverage.” — Fama & French(1992) p. 450

Problem Statement
"The Capital Asset Pricing Model (Sharpe, 1964; Lintner, 1965) predicts that
market beta is the sole determinant of expected stock returns. However, recent
empirical studies have documented several 'anomalies' that contradict this
prediction: the size effect (Banz, 1981), the value effect (Stattman, 1980;
Rosenberg, Reid, and Lanstein, 1985), the leverage effect (Bhandari, 1988), and
the earnings-price ratio effect (Basu, 1983).
These variables are all scaled versions of price, suggesting they may be
redundant proxies for the same underlying risk factors. This study will evaluate
the joint roles of beta, size, leverage, E/P, and book-to-market equity to
determine which variables truly capture the cross-section of expected returns."

Expected Results
Hypothetical Fama-French Proposal
"We hypothesize that many of these variables are redundant. Specifically,
we expect that:
1. Beta will have little or reduced explanatory power when tested alongside
other variables.
2. Some of the current anomaly factors will emerge as the key factors,
absorbing the explanatory power of leverage and E/P.
3. The combination of these key factors will provide a parsimonious model that
captures most of the cross-sectional variation in average returns."

Research Method - The Critical Innovation
The Double-Sort Methodology
"To allow for variation in β that is unrelated to size, we will:
1. First, sort stocks into 10 portfolios based on size (ME)
2. Then, within each size decile, sort into 10 portfolios based on pre-ranking β
This creates 100 portfolios with independent variation in size and β, allowing us to
separate their effects."
Key to Their Contribution!

Research Method - The Critical Innovation
Fama-MacBeth Regressions
"We will use the Fama-MacBeth (1973) cross-sectional regression approach:
1. Each month, run cross-sectional regressions of returns on the variables of 
interest
2. Compute time-series averages of the monthly slopes
3. Use standard errors from the time series to test significance
This allows us to test which variables have non-zero expected premiums."

Varian’s TV Story
A Classic Example: “A Model of Sales”
"I had decided to get a new TV so I followed the ads in the newspaper to get an idea of
how much it would cost. I noticed that the prices fluctuated quite a bit from week to
week. It occurred to me that the challenge to economics was not why the prices were
sometimes low (i.e., during sales) but why they were ever high.”
The insight: Two types of consumers—informed (read ads) and uninformed 
(don't read ads). Stores use sales to price discriminate.
The result: A published paper + a good deal on the TV!

When to Look at the Literature?
Varian’s Advice: Don’t Look Too Soon!
Wait a few weeks before doing a systematic literature review
Practice: You need the experience of developing a model
Originality: If you read others first, your thinking will be shaped by their views
Incubation: Ideas need time to develop—start modeling early
"If you're very lucky and your model is not in the literature, maybe you're wrong. Here is 
where the advisor is really important." — Varian

The Golden Rules
1.
Start with a puzzle – Something that doesn't fit existing theory
2. Ask a clear question – Can you state it in one sentence?
3. Design a test that can reject your hypothesis – Don't just confirm what 
you believe
4. Write a proposal first – Clarify your thinking, get feedback early
5. Check data availability – Before you fall in love with an idea
6. Be parsimonious – The simplest model that captures the essence
